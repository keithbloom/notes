Detail

In-depth analysis

Full reasoning behind the structure above, drawn from industry guidance on showreels and post-production hiring.

Length: keep it under 90 seconds

Sources converge on a short reel: the general rule of thumb sits between 90 seconds and 2 minutes, though several push shorter still — around one minute, with some cutting to just 45 seconds. For entry-level and runner reels specifically, the shorter end is safer. With roughly two dozen pieces to draw from, this forces real curation: a 60–75 second reel realistically fits 5–7 strong shots, not two dozen projects.

The first 5–8 seconds decide everything

This is the most repeated point across every source. Decision-makers often watch only the first 30 seconds, so if the best material isn't front-loaded, attention is lost before it starts. An employer looking through several reels will often watch a few seconds, then sort into "watch again" or "won't" — meaning the opening shot carries more weight than anything else in the reel. The strongest single shot available (most likely from the Same River Twice work) should open, not the school coursework.

Skip long intros — start with the work

Long intros, title cards, and music-only montages before the first shot are a common mistake. Where identifying information is needed, it should be short — name and role only — and layered over footage that's already playing, rather than delaying the work with a static card.

Structure it like a story, with a clear peak

A well-structured reel flows rather than presenting a random sequence of clips. For an editor specifically, a tight montage that shows editing rhythm matters more than any single beautiful shot — which is exactly what the music video's lyric-sync cutting already demonstrates.

Why one focused reel, not a general one

Because the target is specifically post-production, industry guidance is fairly direct: don't combine production and post-production experience in one reel, and don't mix old, low-budget, or university coursework in with the strongest current work. A post-production reel should highlight the most prized work and the relevant techniques — grading, cutting, compositing — rather than breadth for its own sake. At the same time, for someone early-career, making a reel at all (even from student and personal work) demonstrates seriousness about the industry, even without a full professional body of work yet.

Technical specs

  • Format: MP4, H.264 codec
  • Resolution: 1080p or 4K, depending on source footage
  • Frame rate: match the source material (usually 24, 25, or 30fps)
  • Audio: AAC, around –6dB peak levels
  • Aspect ratio: 16:9 for web — also worth preparing square and vertical cuts for social/Instagram sharing
  • Always watch the exported file back before sharing it, to confirm it plays cleanly end to end

What post-production employers specifically look for

Runner and edit-trainee hiring guidance emphasises an interest in how stories are built through assembling shots and scenes, a good eye for composition, attention to detail with fine timings, and genuine curiosity about the technical side of editing tools. None of that requires flashy production value — it rewards a reel that shows deliberate, considered cuts over merely attractive footage.

On re-cutting existing pieces: because this reel is built from already-finished, already-soundtracked work, the biggest practical risk is audio clashing across cuts. A single continuous music bed underneath the whole reel — rather than carrying over each clip's original track — avoids that, and sidesteps licensing questions that come with re-publishing soundtracked material under his own name on a public site.
